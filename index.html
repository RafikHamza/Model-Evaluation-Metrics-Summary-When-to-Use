1/15

What is Machine Learning?

Machine Learning is a way to teach computers to make predictions or decisions based on data, without being explicitly programmed for each specific situation.

Examples:

  • Predicting house prices based on size, location, etc.
  • Identifying if an email is spam or not
  • Recommending movies you might like
  • Recognizing faces in photos

Basic Process:

  1. Collect data
  2. Train a model on that data
  3. Test the model on new data
  4. Evaluate how well it performs ← We'll focus here

Key Point: We need reliable ways to measure if our models are accurate and useful!

2/15

Types of Machine Learning Problems

Regression

Predicting a continuous number

Examples:

  • House price: $250,000
  • Temperature: 72.5°F
  • Sales: 1,523 units

Classification

Predicting a category or class

Examples:

  • Email: Spam or Not Spam
  • Medical: Disease Present or Absent
  • Image: Dog, Cat, or Bird

Each type needs different metrics to evaluate how well the model is performing.

3/15

Why Do We Need Evaluation Metrics?

Metrics help us:

  • Determine if a model is accurate enough to be useful
  • Compare different models to choose the best one
  • Understand what types of errors our model makes
  • Improve our models over time
  • Make responsible decisions about deploying models

Real-world example:

If we build a model to detect cancer from medical images:

  • Is it better to detect 99% of all cancers but have 20% false alarms?
  • Or detect 90% of cancers with only 5% false alarms?

Different metrics help us understand these tradeoffs!

Remember: A model can be "accurate" in one way but perform poorly in another way that matters more for your specific problem.

4/15

Understanding Predictions vs. Reality

Before we dive into specific metrics, let's understand some key terms:

  • y: The actual value (ground truth, what really happened)
  • ŷ: The predicted value from our model (pronounced "y-hat")
  • Error: The difference between prediction and reality (y - ŷ)

Example:

Predicting house prices:

  • Actual price (y): $250,000
  • Model prediction (ŷ): $235,000
  • Error: $15,000 (our model underestimated by $15K)

Different metrics handle these errors in different ways, which we'll explore next.

5/15

Regression Metrics

Used when predicting continuous values like prices, temperatures, or counts.

MetricWhat It MeansExampleGoal
MAE Mean Absolute Error: average of |y - ŷ|
The average size of errors		 Our house price predictions are off by $25,000 on average Smaller
MSE Mean Squared Error: average of (y - ŷ)²
Penalizes large errors more		 Large errors (like $100K off) impact score much more than small errors Smaller
RMSE Root Mean Squared Error: √MSE
Error in original units		 Similar to MAE but gives more weight to large errors Smaller
R² R-squared: How much variance your model explains
Scale: 0 to 1		 R² = 0.75 means your model explains 75% of the variation in house prices Closer to 1

Simple explanation:

If you're predicting house prices, these metrics tell you how much your predictions typically differ from the actual prices, in different ways.

MAE (Mean Absolute Error):

(10K + 20K + 20K + 50K + 10K) ÷ 5 = $22K

"On average, our predictions are off by $22,000"

MSE (Mean Squared Error):

(100M + 400M + 400M + 2500M + 100M) ÷ 5 = 700M

RMSE: √700M = $26.5K

Notice: RMSE ($26.5K) is higher than MAE ($22K) because it penalizes the large $50K error more heavily.

Understanding Classification

Before we explain classification metrics, let's understand what predictions look like:

Example: Medical Test for a Disease

Our model predicts whether a patient has a disease (positive) or not (negative).

For each prediction, there are 4 possible outcomes:

True Positive (TP)

Predicted: Has disease

Reality: Has disease

✓ CORRECT

False Positive (FP)

Predicted: Has disease

Reality: Doesn't have disease

✗ FALSE ALARM

False Negative (FN)

Predicted: No disease

Reality: Has disease

✗ MISSED DETECTION

True Negative (TN)

Predicted: No disease

Reality: Doesn't have disease

✓ CORRECT

8/15

Confusion Matrix Example

Let's say we tested 100 patients, and our model gave these results:

Predicted: Has DiseasePredicted: No DiseaseTotal
Reality: Has Disease 30 (True Positives) 10 (False Negatives) 40 patients actually had disease
Reality: No Disease 20 (False Positives) 40 (True Negatives) 60 patients didn't have disease

Questions we can answer with this data:

  • How many predictions were correct? 30 + 40 = 70 out of 100
  • Of patients who actually had the disease, how many did we catch? 30 out of 40
  • Of patients we flagged as having the disease, how many actually had it? 30 out of 50

Each of these questions corresponds to a different classification metric!

9/15

Classification Metrics

Using our example of 100 patients where: TP=30, FP=20, FN=10, TN=40

MetricFormulaCalculationMeaning
Accuracy (TP+TN)÷Total (30+40)÷100 = 70% Overall, how often is the model correct?
Precision TP÷(TP+FP) 30÷(30+20) = 60% When it predicts "has disease," how often is it right?
Recall (Sensitivity) TP÷(TP+FN) 30÷(30+10) = 75% Of all actual disease cases, what % did it catch?
F1 Score 2×(Prec×Rec)÷(Prec+Rec) 2×(0.6×0.75)÷(0.6+0.75) = 67% Balance between precision and recall

Which metric matters most? It depends on your problem!

  • High precision needed when false positives are costly (e.g., unnecessary surgeries)
  • High recall needed when false negatives are dangerous (e.g., missing a serious disease)
10/15

AUC and ROC Curve - Simplified

Many models don't just give yes/no answers - they give a probability score (like 80% chance of disease).

We need to choose a threshold (e.g., >50% = "positive")

ROC Curve:

Shows tradeoff between:

  • True Positive Rate: % of actual positives caught
  • False Positive Rate: % of negatives incorrectly flagged

It plots performance at all possible thresholds

AUC (Area Under Curve):

Single number measuring overall quality of model

  • AUC = 1.0: Perfect model
  • AUC = 0.5: No better than guessing
  • Most good models: 0.7-0.9

Simple explanation: AUC measures how well your model distinguishes between positive and negative cases, regardless of the specific threshold you choose.

Introduction to Hypothesis Testing

Hypothesis testing helps us determine if there's a real relationship between variables or if observations could be due to random chance.

Null Hypothesis (H0):

"There is no association between variables"

Example: Gender has no effect on preference for tea vs. coffee

Alternative Hypothesis (HA):

"Variables are associated"

Example: Gender does affect preference for tea vs. coffee

p-value: Probability of seeing our results (or more extreme) if null hypothesis were true

  • p < 0.05: Results are statistically significant (reject null)
  • p ≥ 0.05: Not enough evidence to reject null hypothesis

In simple terms: "How likely is it that our observations occurred by random chance?"

Chi-Square Test: Everyday Example

Question: Does age affect preference for social media platforms?

Age GroupPrefers InstagramPrefers TikTokPrefers Twitter
18-25355015
26-40452530

Chi-Square Test:

  1. It compares these observed counts with what we'd expect if age and platform preference were not related
  2. It calculates a χ² value based on differences between observed and expected counts
  3. It provides a p-value indicating the statistical significance

Result: p-value = 0.0001 → Strong evidence that age affects platform preference!

Chi-Square is useful when comparing categorical variables, like:

  • Ad type vs Click/No-click
  • Device type vs Purchase/No-purchase
  • Treatment vs Control for any A/B test

How to Choose the Right Metrics

Step 1: Identify Your Problem Type

  • Regression: Predicting a number
    • Consider: MAE, RMSE, R²
  • Classification: Predicting a category
    • Consider: Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1, AUC

Step 2: Consider the Costs of Errors

  • False Positives costly? Focus on Precision
  • False Negatives costly? Focus on Recall
  • Balance needed? Use F1 Score
  • Overall ranking quality? Use AUC

Real-world examples:

  • Spam filter: High precision (don't block legitimate emails)
  • Cancer screening: High recall (don't miss actual cases)
  • Product recommendations: Balance (show relevant items without overwhelming)

Best Practice: Always report at least two complementary metrics to give a complete picture!
